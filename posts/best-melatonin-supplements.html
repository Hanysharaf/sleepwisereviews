Quick Answer: What Are the Best Melatonin Supplements?

The best melatonin supplement depends on the problem you are solving. For trouble falling asleep, a low-dose immediate-release product is a sensible start (our editorial pick is Life Extension 300mcg), because it is not clear that higher doses work better. For waking in the night, extended-release products such as Natrol are an option, though the evidence for staying asleep is more limited. Prices run from low-cost drugstore tablets to premium brands (see the price comparison below). This is general information, not medical advice. Melatonin can interact with some medications, so ask a doctor or pharmacist first; dosing for children should be set by a pediatrician, and pregnancy or breastfeeding should be discussed with a doctor.

  • Best for falling asleep faster: low-dose (0.5-1mg) immediate-release tablets or sublingual drops, usually taken 30-60 minutes before bed. The average effect on sleep onset is modest.
  • Best for staying asleep through the night: extended/time-release tablets, which are designed to release melatonin gradually over several hours. Evidence for staying asleep is more limited than for falling asleep.
  • Best for people who dislike pills: gummies, though many contain more melatonin than low-dose tablets and the amount can differ from the label. Check the dose and look for a USP or NSF mark.
  • Possibly faster absorption: sublingual or liquid forms, which bypass some digestion, though there is little head-to-head evidence that they work faster in practice.
  • Best for precise dosing: liquid drops, which let you measure a small, specific amount rather than being locked into a fixed tablet strength.
  • Strongest melatonin: stronger is not better. It is not clear that 10mg and higher products work better than low doses, and they may cause more grogginess, headache or dizziness. If you try melatonin, start low.

    How Much Melatonin Do You Need?

    Walk into any pharmacy and the common melatonin strengths are 5mg and 10mg. Trials have used a wide range of doses, from under 1mg to several milligrams, and the evidence on whether higher doses work better for falling asleep is mixed. Lower doses may cause less next-day grogginess, but the research is not precise enough to name a single best dose. A 2013 PLOS ONE meta-analysis of 19 randomized trials found that melatonin shortened the time to fall asleep by about 7 minutes on average, a modest effect.

    Melatonin is sold as a dietary supplement rather than a drug, so products are not approved as sleep medicines and are not required to go through dose-finding trials. The body makes melatonin in very small amounts, which is one reason many researchers suggest starting low. Melatonin is sold as a dietary supplement rather than an approved sleep medicine, and independent testing has found that the amount in some products, including gummies, can differ from the label. Look for a third-party mark such as USP or NSF and treat the label dose as approximate.

    💊 A reasonable starting range: many people begin with 0.5-1mg for trouble falling asleep. Extended-release products are used for night waking, but the evidence there is more limited. It is not clear that higher doses work better, and they may increase side effects such as grogginess, headache or dizziness. Ask a doctor or pharmacist before starting, particularly if you take other medicines.

    When Melatonin Helps (and When It Doesn't)

    Melatonin is a timing signal, not a sedative. Understanding this distinction changes everything about how you use it:

    • It may help: Jet lag, delayed sleep phase (night owls trying to sleep earlier), and some shift workers. The evidence for occasional insomnia is modest
    • It is less likely to help: Chronic insomnia driven by anxiety, stress, or poor sleep habits. These are usually better treated with CBT-I or behavioural changes than with a timing signal
    • It can make things worse: Taken at the wrong time (too early or too late relative to your desired sleep time), it shifts your clock in the wrong direction

    Label directions usually suggest taking melatonin about 30-60 minutes before bed. If you are trying to shift your schedule, the timing is different and a clinician can help you plan it.

    Typical Dose Ranges (General Information)

    Jet lag: a Cochrane review of trials found doses of about 0.5-5mg taken near the destination bedtime were similarly effective, with doses above 5mg not more effective

    Delayed sleep phase: timing is usually earlier in the evening; ask a clinician to plan it

    Waking in the night: extended-release products are sometimes used; evidence is limited

    General sleep onset: many people start at 0.5-1mg, about 30-60 minutes before bed

    Important Safety Notes

    Melatonin is generally considered safe for short-term use in adults. Key considerations:

    • Long-term use: Long-term safety data in people are limited. There is no good human evidence that normal over-the-counter doses reduce the body's own melatonin production or cause dependence, but regular use is worth discussing with a doctor.
    • Interactions: Melatonin can interact with blood thinners, immunosuppressants, and some diabetes medications, among others. Ask a doctor or pharmacist if you take any regular medications.
    • Pregnancy and breastfeeding: Safety data are limited. Talk to a doctor before using melatonin if you are pregnant or breastfeeding.
    • Timing matters: Label directions usually suggest 30-60 minutes before bed. Taking melatonin at the wrong time of day, or trying to shift your clock without guidance, can move your sleep in the wrong direction.
    • Children: Dosing for children should be set by a pediatrician. See our guide to melatonin for kids.
    • Label accuracy: Melatonin is sold as a dietary supplement rather than an approved sleep medicine, and independent testing has found that the amount in some products, including gummies, can differ from the label. Look for a third-party mark such as USP or NSF and treat the label dose as approximate.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    What is the correct melatonin dose for sleep?

    There is no single proven dose. Trials have used a wide range, from under 1mg to several milligrams, and the evidence on whether higher doses work better is mixed and not clear-cut. Many people start low (around 0.5-1mg), and the 5-10mg tablets common in US stores give much higher blood levels than the body makes at night. Higher doses may cause more next-day grogginess, headache or dizziness. Melatonin can interact with some medications, so ask a doctor or pharmacist before starting.

    Can you take melatonin every night?

    Short-term use at low doses appears well tolerated by most adults, and long-term nightly use has limited safety data. There is no good human evidence that normal over-the-counter doses reduce the body's own melatonin production or cause dependence. Some animal and cell studies show melatonin receptors can become less responsive with repeated exposure, but this has not been clearly shown to matter in people at normal doses. If you find you need melatonin to fall asleep every night, talk to a doctor or sleep specialist about the underlying cause and about CBT-I and other behavioral approaches.

    What form of melatonin works fastest?

    Sublingual and liquid forms may be absorbed somewhat faster than standard tablets, but there is little head-to-head evidence and the difference is probably small. Extended-release tablets are designed to release over several hours and are used for night waking, though the evidence there is limited. Label directions usually suggest taking melatonin about 30-60 minutes before bed.

    What is the strongest melatonin, and is stronger better?

    Stronger is not necessarily better. It is not clear that 10mg and higher products work better than lower doses for falling asleep, and higher doses may cause more next-day grogginess, headache or dizziness. A 2013 PLOS ONE meta-analysis of 19 randomized trials (Ferracioli-Oda and colleagues) found that melatonin shortened the time to fall asleep by about 7 minutes on average, a modest effect. If you want to try melatonin, many people start low (around 0.5-1mg). Ask a doctor or pharmacist first if you take other medicines, and ask a pediatrician before giving it to a child.
