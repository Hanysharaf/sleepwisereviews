Wrong: Using Melatonin Like a Sleeping Pill. Right: How Melatonin Actually Works

Melatonin is one of the most widely used sleep supplements in the United States, and it is often used as if it were a sleeping pill. It is not one. It is a timing signal for the body clock with a modest effect on how quickly you fall asleep. This article explains what the research does and does not show about dose, timing, and safety.

    The Wrong Way: Popping 5-10mg Melatonin at Bedtime

    The mistake: Buying a standard 5mg or 10mg melatonin tablet at the pharmacy, taking it right before bed when you want to fall asleep, and expecting it to knock you out like a sleeping pill. This approach misunderstands what melatonin does: the dose is often higher than studies suggest is needed, the timing may not suit your goal, and the mechanism is not sedation.

    Walk into any pharmacy in the United States and the melatonin shelf often includes 5mg, 10mg, or higher strengths, sold as a normal serving size. Melatonin is sold as a dietary supplement in the US, so products are not approved as sleep drugs and are not required to go through the dose-finding trials that drugs do. Common tablet strengths are not clearly tied to dose-finding research.

    As a result, many people take doses that give blood levels well above what the body makes at night, and use melatonin as a sedative, which is not what it is. It is not clear that higher doses work better, and they may cause more next-day grogginess.

    Why This Approach Often Disappoints

    Melatonin is not a sleeping pill. The distinction matters for how you use it. Melatonin is a circadian timing signal. It is produced by the pineal gland in response to darkness, rises in the evening, peaks in the middle of the night, and declines before wake time. It helps signal biological night to the body rather than inducing unconsciousness.

    Sleep researchers commonly describe melatonin as a darkness signal rather than a sleep-inducing drug. It does not appear to build sleep pressure the way time awake does, and its main role is setting the phase of the circadian clock, which shifts when sleep is most likely to occur.

    What the research shows on dose: A 2013 PLOS ONE meta-analysis of 19 randomized trials found that melatonin shortened the time to fall asleep by about 7 minutes on average, a modest effect. Trials have used a wide range of doses, and the evidence on whether higher doses work better is mixed. Reported side effects include headache, dizziness, nausea, and daytime sleepiness.

    A common worry is that regular supplements make the body stop producing its own melatonin, or create a dependency. There is no good human evidence that normal over-the-counter doses reduce the body's own melatonin production or cause dependence. Some animal and cell studies show melatonin receptors can become less responsive with repeated exposure, but this has not been clearly shown to matter in people at normal doses. Long-term safety data in people are limited. There is also no good evidence that melatonin causes withdrawal in the way prescription sleep drugs can.

    Taking more is also not a reliable fix when melatonin seems to stop helping. Higher doses have not been shown to restore the effect and may add grogginess. If you find yourself raising the dose, that is a good reason to talk to a doctor about the underlying sleep problem.

    The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2017 clinical practice guideline on drug treatment of chronic insomnia suggests against using melatonin for it (a weak recommendation), reflecting limited evidence. The stronger evidence for melatonin is in circadian rhythm problems such as delayed sleep phase and jet lag, where the goal is to shift timing rather than induce sleep.

    The Right Way: A Timing Signal at the Correct Dose

    A more cautious approach: If you try melatonin, many people start with a low dose (around 0.5-1mg) rather than 5-10mg, and use it for a defined purpose such as jet lag or shifting a schedule rather than as a nightly sleeping pill. For trouble falling asleep, label directions usually suggest about 30-60 minutes before bed. For shifting your clock earlier, studies often time it earlier in the evening. A clinician can help you plan it.

    Timing depends on your goal. Your body's own melatonin typically starts to rise a couple of hours before habitual sleep onset. Studies of clock shifting generally give melatonin earlier in the evening than people who simply want to fall asleep would take it, because the effect on the clock depends on when it is given. That is why it is worth asking a clinician before using melatonin to shift your schedule.

    The best-supported use is phase-shifting: moving your sleep window earlier, for example in delayed sleep phase. Studies in people with delayed sleep phase have given melatonin hours before the usual bedtime, but doses, timing, and results vary between people, so treat any schedule as a starting point to review with a clinician rather than a fixed prescription.

    For jet lag, a Cochrane review of 10 randomized trials found that melatonin taken close to the target bedtime at the destination reduced jet lag on flights crossing five or more time zones. Doses between 0.5mg and 5mg were similarly effective, with people falling asleep faster and sleeping better after 5mg than after 0.5mg, and doses above 5mg did not appear to be more effective.

    How to Implement This Tonight

    1. Consider a lower dose. If you currently take 5-10mg and get next-day grogginess, a lower dose (around 0.5-1mg) is a reasonable thing to try. It may feel subtler, which is expected because melatonin is a timing signal, not a sedative. Ask a doctor or pharmacist first if you take other medicines.
    2. Pick the timing that fits your goal. For trouble falling asleep, label directions usually suggest about 30-60 minutes before bed. If you are trying to shift your sleep earlier, ask a clinician about timing, because taking it at the wrong time can shift your clock the wrong way.
    3. Pair it with light habits. Evening light suppresses the body's own melatonin, so dimming lights and cutting screen time in the evening supports the same body-clock system melatonin works on. Do not expect a supplement alone to override bright evening light.
    4. Use it for a defined purpose. Melatonin is best suited to short-term goals such as jet lag or shifting your schedule. If you are still relying on it after a couple of months, ask a doctor or sleep specialist about the underlying cause, which may be schedule consistency, light exposure, or insomnia that CBT-I is better placed to treat.     The Science Behind It

    Melatonin is synthesized from serotonin in the pineal gland through a two-step enzymatic process that is gated by darkness. Light — specifically short-wavelength blue light detected by intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells expressing the photopigment melanopsin — suppresses the enzyme AANAT that initiates melatonin synthesis. This is why light exposure at night is so disruptive: it is not just stimulating, it is hormonally blocking the first step of the sleep-signaling cascade.

    The melatonin receptor system in humans involves two high-affinity G-protein coupled receptors: MT1 and MT2. MT1 activation suppresses neuronal firing in the SCN, reducing the alerting drive from the circadian clock. MT2 activation phase-shifts the SCN — this is the mechanism by which the timing of the circadian rhythm is adjusted. How dose maps onto these effects in people is not fully worked out, and it is not clear that higher doses produce better sleep outcomes.

    On long-term effects, the points above apply: the human evidence for dependence or reduced natural production is lacking, and long-term safety data are limited. That is one more reason to use melatonin for a defined purpose and to talk to a doctor about regular use.

    Safety Notes

    Melatonin can interact with some medications, including blood thinners, some diabetes medications and immunosuppressants, so ask a doctor or pharmacist before starting it, especially if you are pregnant, breastfeeding or taking other medicines. Dosing for children should be set by a pediatrician. See our guide to melatonin for kids before giving it to a child.

    Melatonin is sold as a dietary supplement rather than an approved sleep medicine, and independent testing has found that the amount in some products, including gummies, can differ from the label. Look for a third-party mark such as USP or NSF and treat the label dose as approximate.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    What is the correct dose of melatonin?

    There is no single proven dose. Trials have used a wide range, from under 1mg to several milligrams, and the evidence on whether higher doses work better is mixed and not clear-cut. Many people start low (around 0.5-1mg), and the 5-10mg tablets common in US stores give much higher blood levels than the body makes at night. Higher doses may cause more next-day grogginess, headache or dizziness. Melatonin can interact with some medications, so ask a doctor or pharmacist before starting.

    When should you take melatonin?

    For trouble falling asleep, label directions usually suggest about 30-60 minutes before bed. For jet lag or a delayed body clock, studies time it differently, often earlier in the evening or at the destination bedtime, so it helps to ask a clinician to plan it. Taking melatonin at the wrong time of day can shift your body clock in the wrong direction.

    Is melatonin a sedative?

    No. It is a circadian timing signal that tells the body it is nighttime — it does not directly induce unconsciousness. Its strongest evidence is for circadian issues like jet lag and shift work, not as a general nightly sleep aid.

    Can taking too much melatonin cause dependency?

    There is no good human evidence that normal over-the-counter doses reduce the body's own melatonin production or cause dependence. Some animal and cell studies show melatonin receptors can become less responsive with repeated exposure, but this has not been clearly shown to matter in people at normal doses. Long-term safety data in people are limited. Higher doses are not clearly better and may cause more grogginess, so ask a doctor or pharmacist about regular use, especially alongside other medicines.
