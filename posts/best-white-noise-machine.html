What the Research Says About White Noise and Sleep

White noise may help some people through auditory masking: it raises the background sound level so sudden noises stand out less. Studies on whether this improves sleep are mixed, often small and of low quality, so white noise is not shown to help everyone.

  • Auditory masking: The brain responds more to sudden changes in sound than to constant sound. A steady background sound can make a door closing or traffic stand out less, which is the main reason people use these machines.
  • Falling asleep: Some people find a constant, neutral sound helps them settle. Research findings are inconsistent, and some people notice no difference or prefer silence.
  • Pink noise and deep sleep: A small 2017 study in Frontiers in Human Neuroscience tested short pink-noise bursts timed to slow brain waves in a laboratory setting. That is different from running a continuous machine all night, so do not assume the results carry over to home use.
  • Babies and white noise: Some parents use white noise for infants, but the evidence is limited and there is a hearing-safety concern: a sound machine run loudly or placed close to a crib can reach volumes that may be a risk to an infant's hearing. Keep the volume low, place the machine as far from the crib as possible, and ask your pediatrician.
  • Tinnitus: Background sound is commonly used to mask tinnitus (ringing in the ears), and some people find it helps at bedtime. If you have ongoing tinnitus, talk to a doctor or audiologist.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between white noise, pink noise, and brown noise?

White noise contains equal energy across all frequencies and sounds like static or hissing. Pink noise has more energy in lower frequencies and sounds like a fan or gentle rain. Brown noise has even more low-frequency energy, sounding like deep rushing water or strong wind. All three can mask disruptive sounds. Research on pink noise and deep sleep is limited and does not show that it works better than other sounds for everyone.

Can white noise machines damage hearing?

Any sound machine can contribute to hearing damage if it is too loud for too long. The National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders (NIDCD) notes that regular exposure to sounds at or above 85 decibels can damage hearing over time, so keep the volume as low as still masks the noise. Infants need extra care: studies of infant sound machines have found that some can reach high volumes when turned up close to a crib. Keep the machine as far from the crib as possible, at a low volume, and ask your pediatrician about using one.

Fan-based vs. digital white noise machine — which is better?

Fan-based machines (like Marpac Dohm) produce natural, organic sound from actual air movement. Digital machines (like LectroFan) offer more sound variety, consistent volume control, and no moving parts to fail. For pure white noise, both can produce steady masking noise. For sound variety or specific sounds like rain or ocean, digital is better. Fan-based is preferred by those sensitive to slight sound loops.

Where should I place a white noise machine in the bedroom?

Many people place the machine a few feet from the bed, pointed toward the main noise source (door, window), rather than right next to the head. For babies, place it as far from the crib as possible and keep the volume low. The aim is to mask the noise without making the machine the dominant sound in the room.

Can I use a white noise app instead of a machine?

Phone apps can work, especially for occasional use or travel. Dedicated machines have some advantages: they free your phone from running all night and from notifications, and a dedicated speaker may sound better than a phone speaker. Which is better is largely a personal preference.
