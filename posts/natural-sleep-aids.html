Best Natural Sleep Aids for Adults (2026): What the Evidence Shows

The supplement aisle is full of sleep aids promising a better night. Here is what the research says about each one, how strong that evidence is, and what to watch out for — starting with the best-supported option, which is not a supplement at all.

The quality of evidence behind natural sleep aids varies enormously. A few have been studied in many controlled trials; others have only small, short studies, or little human research at all. This guide summarizes published research and professional guidance. We have not run our own tests on these products, and nothing here replaces advice from your doctor.

Quick answer: There is no single best natural sleep aid for adults. For ongoing (chronic) insomnia, the best-supported option is cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), which major sleep and medical guidelines recommend first — see our CBT-I guide. Among supplements, melatonin has the most research, with a modest effect that is clearest for jet lag, shift work and delayed sleep timing. Talk to a doctor first if you take medication, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or have had trouble sleeping for more than a few weeks.

    CBT-I: The Best-Supported Option for Chronic Insomnia

    If your trouble sleeping has lasted more than a few weeks, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the option with the strongest evidence. Major sleep and medical guidelines recommend it as the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia, ahead of sleeping pills and supplements. It works on the habits, schedule and worries that keep insomnia going, and the benefits tend to last after the program ends. It can be delivered by a clinician, a digital program or a book; sleep restriction, one of its components, can make you sleepier for the first couple of weeks, so guided help is useful.     Natural Sleep Aids, Ranked by Strength of Evidence

    1

    Melatonin

    Evidence strengthMixed: modest overall, clearer for circadian problems

    Melatonin is a hormone your body makes in response to darkness. It signals timing rather than acting as a sedative. Meta-analyses of controlled trials generally find a small reduction in the time it takes to fall asleep — a matter of minutes for many people — and the effect is clearest for circadian problems such as jet lag, shift work and delayed sleep timing. Guidance from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine is more supportive for those uses than for chronic insomnia, where it has been cautious about routine use.

    Supplements are not tightly regulated, so strength and purity vary between brands. Short-term use appears safe for most adults, but long-term safety is less well studied. See our melatonin guide and best melatonin supplements.

    Doses used in studies vary widely. Check the label and ask a doctor, especially if you take other medicines.
    2

    Magnesium (glycinate)

    Evidence strengthLimited / mixed

    Magnesium is an essential mineral, and the glycinate form is the one most often sold for sleep. A small trial in older adults with insomnia reported better sleep measures, but systematic reviews have rated the overall evidence as low quality, and it is unclear whether people who are not deficient benefit. Whether glycinate works better than other forms has not been established.

    Too much supplemental magnesium can cause diarrhea and stomach upset. People with kidney disease, and those taking certain antibiotics or osteoporosis medicines, should check with a doctor first. See our magnesium glycinate for sleep page.

    The NIH upper limit for supplemental magnesium in adults is 350 mg per day. Follow the label and ask a doctor if unsure.
    3

    Valerian Root

    Evidence strengthMixed: results are inconsistent

    Valerian is one of the oldest sleep remedies in Western herbalism, and studies disagree. Some report small improvements in sleep quality; others find no difference from placebo. Reviews generally describe the evidence as inconsistent, and products differ in how they are made, which makes results hard to compare.

    Side effects are usually mild (headache, dizziness, stomach upset), though rare liver problems have been reported. Avoid combining valerian with alcohol or sedating medicines. See valerian root supplements.

    Doses vary by product. Follow the label and ask a doctor if you take sedating medicine.
    4

    Tart Cherry

    Evidence strengthLimited: small studies

    Tart cherry juice and capsules contain small amounts of melatonin and plant compounds. A few small, short studies reported modest gains in sleep time or insomnia severity, but the studies are small and the results are not conclusive. A glass of juice also adds sugar and calories.

    See tart cherry supplements for product options.

    Study doses and products differ. Check the label for sugar and serving size.
    5

    Glycine

    Evidence strengthLimited: small studies

    Glycine is an amino acid. Small studies, largely from Japan and in people who reported poor sleep, found better subjective sleep quality with about 3 g taken before bed. Larger confirming trials are lacking, so treat the findings as early. It was generally well tolerated in those studies.

    If you take psychiatric medication, ask a doctor before trying it. See glycine supplements.

    Studies used about 3 g before bed. Ask a doctor first if you take other medicines.
    6

    L-Theanine

    Evidence strengthLimited: few small trials

    L-theanine is an amino acid found in tea. It has mainly been studied for relaxation and stress, with sleep as a secondary outcome. A small randomized trial in healthy adults reported better sleep quality scores with 200 mg daily, but there are few trials and long-term data are limited.

    See L-theanine supplements.

    Studies have used around 200 mg. Ask a doctor first if you take medication.
    7

    Ashwagandha

    Evidence strengthLimited: small, short trials

    Ashwagandha is an herb from Ayurvedic medicine, studied mainly for stress. Several small, short randomized trials of standardized extracts reported better sleep measures, but many used the same few extracts, sample sizes were small, and long-term safety is not established.

    Rare cases of liver injury have been reported, it should be avoided in pregnancy, and it may affect thyroid tests or medication. See ashwagandha for sleep.

    Doses differ between extracts. Follow the label and ask a doctor first.
    8

    Lavender

    Evidence strengthLimited: small, varied studies

    Lavender aroma, and a standardized oral lavender oil made for internal use, have been studied for anxiety and sleep in small trials. Results hint at a possible small benefit, but studies are small and use different products, so confidence is low.

    Aromatherapy is low-risk for most people, though it can irritate skin or trigger headache or allergy. Do not swallow essential oils unless the product is made for oral use.

    No established sleep dose. Follow the product label.
    9

    Passionflower

    Evidence strengthWeak: very few trials

    Passionflower (Passiflora incarnata) is a calming herb often drunk as tea. One small trial of passionflower tea over a week reported better subjective sleep than placebo; evidence beyond that is thin. It is generally considered low-risk, but it can cause drowsiness and should not be stacked with other sedating herbs.

    No established sleep dose. Follow the product label.
    10

    Lemon Balm

    Evidence strengthWeak: small studies, often combined with valerian

    Lemon balm is a mild calming herb, most often sold in combination with valerian. Because it is usually studied in combination, it is hard to credit any effect to lemon balm itself, and the studies that exist are small and short. It is a pleasant tea ritual, but the evidence does not support confident claims about sleep.

    No established sleep dose. Follow the product label.
    11

    5-HTP

    Evidence strengthWeak for sleep; caution advised

    5-HTP is a building block the body uses to make serotonin, and serotonin can be turned into melatonin. That is the theory; human evidence for sleep specifically is thin. 5-HTP can interact with antidepressants and other serotonin-affecting medicines, so do not take it with those unless a doctor advises it.

    Long-term safety is not well studied.

    No established sleep dose. Talk to a doctor first, especially if you take any medication.
    12

    CBD (Cannabidiol)

    Evidence strengthWeak: hype exceeds evidence

    CBD gets a lot of attention as a sleep aid, but clinical evidence for sleep is preliminary and inconsistent. Studies have mostly looked at anxiety or specific conditions, with doses and products that differ widely. The FDA has not approved CBD for insomnia, product quality varies, and CBD can cause drowsiness and interact with other medicines.

    No established sleep dose. Talk to a doctor before using it.

    How to Use Natural Sleep Aids More Safely

    • Start with the basics: a regular sleep and wake time, less caffeine and alcohol (alcohol fragments sleep), and a calmer evening routine. See the sleep hygiene checklist.
    • Change one thing at a time so you can tell what helps, and stop if you notice side effects.
    • Do not stack several sedating herbs (such as valerian, passionflower and lemon balm), and do not mix them with alcohol or sedating medicines.
    • Check with a doctor or pharmacist first if you take any medication, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or have liver or kidney disease.
    • Remember that supplements are not tightly regulated. Strength and purity vary between brands, so look for products tested by an independent lab.

    When to See a Doctor

    See a doctor if trouble sleeping lasts more than a few weeks, if you snore loudly or others notice you stop breathing at night, if you are very sleepy during the day, or if you are thinking about taking a supplement long term. Insomnia can have treatable causes such as sleep apnea, restless legs, anxiety, depression, pain or the side effects of medication, and a supplement will not fix those.

    💚 Where to start: If sleep has been a problem for more than a few weeks, begin with sleep habits and CBT-I, and talk to a doctor. If you want to try a supplement, change one thing at a time and stop if you notice side effects.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    What is the best natural sleep aid for adults?

    There is no single best one. For chronic insomnia, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the best-supported option and is recommended first by major sleep and medical guidelines. Among supplements, melatonin has the most research, but its effect on falling asleep is modest and is clearest for jet lag, shift work and delayed sleep timing. Magnesium, valerian, tart cherry, glycine and L-theanine have limited or inconsistent evidence. Talk to a doctor if you take medication or have had trouble sleeping for more than a few weeks.

    What is the strongest natural sleep aid?

    No natural remedy is clearly the strongest, and none works like a prescription sedative. Melatonin is the most studied supplement, but studies generally find a small effect. Being natural does not make a remedy risk-free: valerian, ashwagandha, 5-HTP and CBD can cause side effects or interact with medicines. If sleep problems persist, CBT-I has better evidence than any supplement, so ask a doctor.

    What is a good natural sleep aid for anxiety?

    No supplement has been proven to treat anxiety-related sleep problems. Small studies have looked at L-theanine, ashwagandha and lavender for stress or anxiety, with limited or mixed results. Anxiety that disrupts sleep is worth discussing with a doctor, because CBT-I and treatments for anxiety itself have stronger evidence. See our guide to sleep aids for anxiety.

    Is melatonin safe for long-term use?

    Short-term use appears safe for most adults, according to the NIH's National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health. Long-term safety is less well studied, so it is reasonable to use melatonin for a specific purpose and review it with a doctor, especially if you take other medicines, are pregnant or are breastfeeding. Supplements are not tightly regulated, so the amount in a product can differ from the label.

    Does magnesium help with sleep?

    Possibly, but the evidence is limited. A small trial in older adults with insomnia reported benefit, but systematic reviews have rated the overall evidence as low quality. Glycinate is the form usually marketed for sleep, but it has not been shown to work better than other forms. The NIH sets 350 mg per day as the upper limit for supplemental magnesium in adults, and too much can cause diarrhea. Check with a doctor if you have kidney disease or take other medicines.

    When should I see a doctor about sleep?

    See a doctor if trouble sleeping lasts more than a few weeks, if you snore loudly or stop breathing at night, if you are very sleepy during the day, or if you are thinking about taking a supplement long term. Insomnia can have treatable causes such as sleep apnea, restless legs, anxiety, depression, pain or medication side effects.
