Quick answer: Research on tart cherry for sleep is limited to a few small, short studies, so treat it as something to try, not a proven treatment. Most of those studies used juice or concentrate, so those forms have the most research behind them; capsules and gummies are more convenient but less studied. If you want label verification, look for a USP or NSF mark. For ongoing insomnia, CBT-I has much stronger support (see our CBT-I guide and the sleep aids evidence hub).

Small studies, including a 2012 trial in the European Journal of Nutrition, have reported modest sleep improvements from tart cherry juice. The studies were small and short, and the results need confirming in larger trials. Tart cherry has not been shown to treat insomnia.

Tart cherry products usually come from Prunus cerasus, often the Montmorency variety, which is also the variety named in some of the studies. Sweet cherries are a different fruit. Studies used juice or concentrate; there is little research on capsules, powders or gummies.

What the Research Shows

Why people try it

Tart cherries contain small amounts of melatonin and plant compounds called anthocyanins. Some researchers have proposed that these compounds might support the body's own melatonin, but this is a hypothesis rather than an established mechanism in people.

Evidence summary

Evidence is limited. A small 2012 trial in the European Journal of Nutrition and a few other small, short studies, some in older adults and some in people with insomnia, reported modest improvements in sleep measures with tart cherry juice. The studies used juice or concentrate rather than capsules or gummies, were small, and have not been confirmed in large trials. The effect on sleep is uncertain.

Tart cherry has also been studied for muscle soreness and inflammation. That is separate from sleep, and we make no claims about it here.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does tart cherry help with sleep?

It may help some people, but the evidence is limited. Tart cherries contain small amounts of melatonin and plant compounds called anthocyanins, and a few small, short studies of tart cherry juice reported modest improvements in sleep. Larger trials are needed, and no tart cherry product is approved to treat insomnia.

What dose of tart cherry is effective for sleep?

There is no established dose. Small studies used juice or concentrate, in some cases twice a day, but capsules, powders and gummies vary widely in how much tart cherry they contain. Follow the product label, and ask a doctor or pharmacist if you take medicines or have diabetes, because juice and concentrate contain natural sugars.

Is Montmorency tart cherry the only variety that works?

Not necessarily. Montmorency is the variety named in several of the small studies, and it is a tart cherry (Prunus cerasus). Other tart cherry types and sweet cherries have been studied far less, so we cannot say how they compare. Check the label for the variety if it matters to you.

How long does tart cherry take to work for sleep?

There is no reliable timeline. The small studies ran for days to a couple of weeks and reported modest results. If you try it and notice no change after a couple of weeks, it is reasonable to stop. See a doctor if sleep problems last more than a few weeks.

Can I take tart cherry with melatonin?

Ask a doctor or pharmacist first. Tart cherry contains a small amount of melatonin, and there is no good evidence on combining it with melatonin supplements, so we cannot say whether it helps or is safe for you. Melatonin can interact with some medicines.

What works best for chronic insomnia?

Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the best-supported option and is recommended first by the American College of Physicians and in American Academy of Sleep Medicine guidance. Supplements such as tart cherry have far less evidence behind them.
