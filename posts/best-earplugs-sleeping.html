Quick Answer: What Are the Best Earplugs for Sleeping?

The best earplugs for sleeping are slim, low-profile foam or soft silicone plugs that seal the canal without protruding. Our picks: Mack's Slim Fit (NRR 29) for best overall and side sleepers, Howard Leight MAX-1 (NRR 33) for maximum noise blocking, Loop Quiet (SNR 27) for reusable comfort, and 3M E-A-R Classic (NRR 29) for the lowest cost per night. A higher NRR also means you will hear less of an alarm, so pick the lowest rating that solves your noise problem. Ratings are manufacturer-stated; see the price and rating comparison below.

  • Best for side sleepers: flush, low-profile designs (slim foam or reusable silicone loops) that don't press against the pillow.
  • Best for blocking snoring: the highest-NRR foam plugs (NRR 32–33) — they block the most noise, with the alarm-awareness tradeoff noted below.
  • Best reusable: soft silicone loop-style plugs — washable, lower waste, and gentle over a full night.
  • Best budget / disposable: standard foam plugs in bulk — the lowest cost per night when inserted correctly.

Why Noise Ruins Sleep More Than You Think

Noise is one of the most common environmental complaints among poor sleepers. Research suggests that sounds you do not consciously wake to can still cause brief arousals and lighter sleep, although how much this matters varies from person to person. Traffic, a snoring partner or early-morning construction are typical culprits. Earplugs are one fix; a white noise machine or a quieter sleep environment are others.

The World Health Organization's environmental noise guidelines recommend keeping average night-time noise outside the bedroom below about 40 decibels, because long-term exposure above that level has been linked with sleep disturbance and other health effects. Many city bedrooms exceed that level.

🔇 What NRR means: Noise Reduction Rating (NRR) is a lab rating in decibels, printed on US hearing protection. Real-world protection is usually lower than the label because of fit and insertion, so treat it as a ceiling, not a promise. NRR and SNR (the European rating used for Loop) are measured differently and should not be compared directly.

The Three Earplug Problems for Side Sleepers

Comfort overnight is a different test from comfort at a desk. The problems that matter most:

  • Pressure pain: Foam earplugs pressed against a pillow for 8 hours create real discomfort — especially with standard cylindrical plugs. Low-profile or flanged designs avoid this.
  • Falling out: Foam plugs that aren't inserted correctly can work loose during the night. Rolling and inserting them properly helps.
  • Over-isolation: Some people find heavy sound blocking disorienting. Lower-rated plugs reduce rather than block noise for these sleepers.
  • Safety awareness: Earplugs also muffle smoke alarms, a crying child or someone calling for help. If that matters in your home, choose a lower NRR and test that you can still hear your alarm.

How to Insert Foam Earplugs Correctly

Most people don't get the noise reduction they expect because they don't insert foam earplugs properly. The correct technique:

  1. Roll: Compress the earplug into a thin cylinder by rolling between your fingers for 10–15 seconds
  2. Pull: Reach over your head with the opposite hand and pull your ear upward and outward — this straightens the ear canal
  3. Insert: Push the compressed earplug deep into the canal while still holding the ear pulled
  4. Hold: Keep gentle pressure on the plug for 20–30 seconds while it expands — don't release until it has fully sealed

A correctly inserted earplug feels snug and reduces your voice to a muffled sound. If it still sounds almost normal, it hasn't sealed properly — remove and repeat.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are earplugs safe to use every night?

Many people use earplugs nightly without problems, but there are tradeoffs. Regular use can contribute to earwax buildup and irritation, and plugs reduce how well you hear alarms, a child or a smoke detector. Use them when you need them, keep them clean, and stop and see a doctor if you notice pain, discharge or changes in your hearing.

What NRR rating is best for sleeping?

There is no single right number. NRR is a lab rating from the manufacturer, and real-world protection is usually lower than the label because of fit, so treat it as an upper bound. Many sleepers do well with a mid-range plug (roughly NRR 22 to 29) that they can wear comfortably all night. The highest-rated foam plugs (NRR 33) block the most noise but also make it harder to hear alarms.

Do earplugs actually block snoring?

They can reduce snoring noticeably, but complete silence is unlikely. A well-fitted high-NRR foam plug usually blocks the most, while low-profile silicone plugs trade some noise reduction for comfort when you sleep on your side. Fit matters more than the number on the box. See our guide to earplugs for a snoring partner for more detail.

What earplugs are best for side sleepers?

Low-profile designs that sit flush with the ear tend to be more comfortable against a pillow. Slim foam plugs such as Mack's Slim Fit and reusable silicone plugs such as Loop Quiet are the usual choices. Wider plugs can press on the ear when you lie on your side.

Can you hear a smoke alarm with earplugs in?

Possibly not as well. Earplugs lower the volume of a smoke or fire alarm along with everything else, and a sleeping person may not wake to a quieter alarm. If you wear earplugs every night, consider a lower-NRR plug, interconnected alarms, or an alarm with a strobe or bed shaker, and check that you can hear your alarm with your plugs in.

Do foam earplugs work better than silicone?

Foam plugs generally carry higher NRR ratings (up to 33) because they expand to fill the canal. Reusable silicone plugs are usually lower-profile and need no rolling, which many side sleepers find more comfortable. For maximum noise reduction, foam usually wins; for comfort, silicone often does.

Can earplugs cause ear infections?

They can contribute to irritation or infection, especially if plugs are dirty, shared or pushed in roughly. Use disposable foam plugs once, clean reusable plugs as the manufacturer directs, and see a doctor if you have ear pain, discharge or recurring infections.
