Best Sleep Supplements in 2026: 7 Compared (Beyond Melatonin)

Melatonin gets the most attention, but it is only one of many sleep supplements. We compared seven popular options by type, typical dose range, price and how much research supports them, and we are upfront about where that research is thin. None is approved to treat insomnia, and the evidence differs a lot by ingredient.

Quick Answer: What's the Best Sleep Supplement?

There is no single best sleep supplement, and none is proven to work for everyone. Melatonin is the most studied, but its effect is modest and clearest for jet lag, shift work and delayed sleep timing. Magnesium, L-theanine, glycine, ashwagandha and GABA have limited or early evidence, and valerian results are inconsistent. For chronic insomnia, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the best-supported option and is recommended first by the American College of Physicians (see our CBT-I guide). Supplements are not FDA-approved for treating insomnia, label accuracy varies, and interactions are possible, so ask a doctor or pharmacist first.

  • Most studied: melatonin - modest effect, clearest for jet lag, shift work and delayed sleep timing. There is no single proven dose.
  • Commonly tried, limited evidence: magnesium glycinate, L-theanine, glycine and ashwagandha - small or early studies.
  • Inconsistent results: valerian root - some trials show benefit, others do not.
  • Deep sleep: no supplement here has strong evidence for increasing it - see the deep sleep section.
  • Best-supported overall: CBT-I, a non-drug program. Our sleep aids evidence hub shows how the options compare.

    What Melatonin Does (and Does Not Do)

    Melatonin is a hormone your body makes in response to darkness. It works more like a timing signal than a sedative. Reviews of controlled trials generally find a small reduction in the time it takes to fall asleep, and the clearest benefit is for circadian problems such as jet lag, shift work and delayed sleep timing. Many people expect it to work like a sleeping pill, which is one reason it often disappoints. See our melatonin guide and best melatonin supplements for more.

    The other supplements below are proposed to work in different ways, but for most of them the human evidence is limited. This guide covers seven popular options, organized by what people commonly use them for, and notes where the research is thin.

    ⚠️ Medical disclaimer: Sleep supplements are not FDA-approved for treating insomnia, can interact with medications, and are not appropriate for everyone. Label accuracy varies between products. Consult a healthcare provider before starting any new supplement, especially if you take prescription medications or have underlying health conditions.

    How to Choose: Matching Supplement to Sleep Problem

    If your problem is falling asleep (racing mind)

    Behavioral approaches such as a consistent wind-down routine and CBT-I have stronger evidence than any supplement. If you want to try a supplement, L-theanine and magnesium are commonly chosen; evidence for both is limited. It is sensible to try one at a time at the label dose and to ask a doctor or pharmacist about interactions.

    If your problem is staying asleep (waking at 2–4am)

    Waking in the early hours has many possible causes, including stress, alcohol, sleep apnea, pain and medicines. Supplements such as glycine have only small studies behind them. If it keeps happening, see a doctor rather than adding supplements.

    If your problem is stress-driven insomnia

    Ashwagandha has been studied mainly for stress, with small trials. Because it is not suitable for everyone, ask a doctor or pharmacist first. CBT-I and treatments for stress and anxiety have stronger evidence.

    If your problem is schedule misalignment (travel, shift work)

    Melatonin is the most studied supplement for jet lag, shift work and delayed sleep timing, and timing matters. Bright light at the right time also affects the body clock; a clinician can help plan both.

    Is There a Supplement for Deep Sleep?

    Not one with strong evidence. Many people search for a deep sleep supplement, but no supplement in this guide has strong evidence for increasing deep (slow-wave) sleep. Studies of sleep supplements usually measure how long it takes to fall asleep or self-rated sleep quality rather than sleep stages. Deep sleep tends to decline with age and is influenced by things like alcohol, caffeine, a regular sleep schedule and untreated sleep disorders such as sleep apnea. If you rarely feel rested, talk to a doctor. See also our sleep aids evidence hub.

    💡 Where to start: If trouble sleeping has lasted more than a few weeks, start with a doctor and consider CBT-I, which has stronger evidence than any supplement. If you decide to try a supplement, pick one, use the label dose, and ask a doctor or pharmacist first, especially if you take other medicines.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Which sleep supplement is most effective?

    No supplement is clearly the most effective. Melatonin is the most studied, but its effect is modest and is clearest for jet lag, shift work and delayed sleep timing. Magnesium, L-theanine, glycine, ashwagandha and GABA have limited or early evidence, and valerian results are inconsistent. For chronic insomnia, CBT-I has stronger evidence than any supplement and is recommended first by the American College of Physicians. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine suggests against using diphenhydramine (found in many over-the-counter sleep aids) to treat insomnia; ask a doctor or pharmacist.

    Are sleep supplements safe long-term?

    Long-term safety is not well established for most sleep supplements, and supplements are not tightly regulated, so the amount in a product can differ from the label. The NIH sets 350 mg per day as the upper limit for supplemental magnesium in adults. Melatonin appears safe for short-term use in most adults, but long-term safety is less well studied. Ashwagandha and valerian have cautions and can interact with some medicines. Review any supplement you take regularly with a doctor or pharmacist. Prescription sleep medicines have their own risks, including dependence for some, and should be used under a clinician's guidance.

    Should I take sleep supplements before or after a meal?

    Directions vary by product, so follow the label. Magnesium can upset the stomach in some people, and some labels suggest taking it with food. For other supplements, ask a pharmacist whether timing or food matters for you, especially if you take other medicines.

    Is there a supplement for deep sleep?

    No supplement in this guide has strong evidence for increasing deep (slow-wave) sleep. Studies of sleep supplements usually measure how long it takes to fall asleep or self-rated sleep quality, not sleep stages. Deep sleep tends to decline with age and is influenced by things like alcohol, caffeine, a regular sleep schedule and untreated sleep disorders such as sleep apnea. If you rarely feel rested, talk to a doctor.
