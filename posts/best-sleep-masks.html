Quick Answer: What's the Best Sleep Mask?

The best sleep mask depends on how you sleep and how dark you need the room to be. Contoured, cupped masks are designed to give the closest thing to total blackout and keep pressure off your eyelids, while flat fabric masks are cheaper and cooler but tend to leak more light around the nose. Our picks: Manta for best overall and side sleepers, Nidra Deep Rest for a budget contoured design, and Alaska Bear Silk for a low-cost flat mask. Evidence note: hospital-based studies of eye masks are mildly supportive, but evidence for ordinary home use is limited. See the price comparison below.

  • Best for total blackout: contoured/molded cup masks — the eye cups float above the eyelids and seal at the edges, designed to block most light.
  • Best for side sleepers: contoured cup masks or well-fitted flat silk masks — anything that won't dig into your face when you turn.
  • Best for hot sleepers: flat silk or satin masks — breathable fabric with no foam trapping heat.
  • Best for travel: lightweight flat fabric masks — they pack flat and weigh almost nothing.
  • Best budget option: flat fabric masks — the lowest cost per mask, though light control is weaker than contoured designs.

    Why Darkness Matters More Than You Think

    Light is one of the main signals the body clock uses. Research suggests that light at night can suppress melatonin and may make it harder to fall or stay asleep, although the effect depends on brightness, timing and the person. Closed eyelids block some light but not all of it, which is the idea behind a sleep mask.

    This is the problem a sleep mask is meant to solve, and it is why design matters. A mask that leaks light around the nose bridge, shifts during the night, or presses on your eyelids may cancel out the benefit you are looking for.

    What to Look For in a Sleep Mask

    1. True Blackout vs. Light Reduction

    There's a meaningful difference between "reducing light" and achieving genuine blackout. Test any mask with this method: put it on, then hold your phone torch close to different areas of the mask. Any glow you see through the fabric or around the edges is light reaching your retinas during sleep.

    2. Nose Bridge Design

    The nose bridge is where many flat masks leak light. Unless the mask has a structured nose piece or contoured cups that create a seal below your eyes, light will find its way in from the bottom. It is worth checking before you buy.

    3. Strap Adjustment and Hair Safety

    A strap that's too tight creates pressure headaches. Too loose and the mask shifts. Look for adjustable elastic with a velcro or slider mechanism, and avoid anything with a rough edge that can snag fine hair.

    4. Material and Breathability

    Silk and satin are the most breathable and gentlest on skin. Memory foam and TEMPUR foam provide better blackout but trap more heat — a concern if you already sleep hot. Synthetic microfiber is light and cheap but less durable.

    🔬 Science note: The retina contains light-sensitive cells called intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs) that help the body clock respond to light. Closed eyelids block some but not all light, and research suggests light at night can affect melatonin and sleep. How much depends on brightness, timing and the person.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Do sleep masks actually improve sleep quality?

    They may help some people, but the evidence is limited. Studies in hospital and intensive-care settings, where eye masks were usually used together with earplugs, have been mildly supportive, while evidence for ordinary home use is limited. Light, including some light that passes through closed eyelids, can affect melatonin, so a mask is a reasonable low-cost thing to try if light disturbs your sleep. It is not a proven treatment for sleep problems.

    What makes a good sleep mask?

    Look for a design that limits light entering at the sides: a nose bridge or contoured cups help. Contoured masks with eye cups (like the Manta or Nidra) keep pressure off the eyelids and leave room for your eyes to move. Silk is cool and gentle on skin, while memory foam contoured masks tend to block the most light. Choose a lightweight mask with a strap that is snug but not tight.

    Can sleep masks cause eye problems?

    Sleep masks are generally considered safe for most people when they fit comfortably. A mask that presses on your eyes can be uncomfortable, and contoured or cupped designs that leave space around the eyes avoid that. If you have an eye condition such as glaucoma, or you get eye irritation, ask your eye doctor before wearing a mask every night.

    How do I wash a sleep mask without ruining it?

    Check the care label first, since fabric and foam masks differ. Silk and satin masks generally do best with gentle hand washing in cool water and air drying flat -- machine drying can break down the fibers and elastic. Contoured foam masks usually have a removable, washable cover so the foam core stays dry. Washing regularly matters because oils and sweat build up against your skin every night.
