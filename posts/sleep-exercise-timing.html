The Best Time to Exercise for Better Sleep

Exercise improves sleep quality - but timing matters. Morning workouts advance your circadian rhythm while evening workouts can delay sleep onset.

    Exercise and Sleep: The Connection

    The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans say adults who are more physically active sleep better. The evidence on the best time of day is less settled than the evidence for being active at all, so this guide separates what is well supported from what is not.

    Morning Exercise (6am-10am)

    • In one study of 60-minute moderate treadmill sessions, exercise at about 7 a.m. shifted melatonin timing earlier (a phase advance). The study measured body-clock timing, not sleep
    • The NHS suggests being active in the morning or early afternoon rather than close to bedtime

    Afternoon Exercise (2pm-6pm)

    • The same study found phase advances for exercise from about 1 p.m. to 4 p.m., with minimal shifts around 4 p.m.
    • The NHLBI advises regular activity during the daytime, at least 5 to 6 hours before going to bed

    Evening Exercise (After 7pm)

    • A 2019 meta-analysis found no overall negative effect of evening exercise on sleep in healthy people, though vigorous exercise ending an hour or less before bed might impair sleep onset, total sleep time and sleep efficiency
    • The NHS advises avoiding exercise in the 4 hours before bed, and the NHLBI says exercising close to bedtime can make it harder to fall asleep. The advice varies, so test what works for you
    💪 Practical takeaway: Regular activity is the best-supported habit, and the timing evidence is mixed. Pick a time you can stick to and watch how your own sleep responds.

    What the Research Says About Exercise and Sleep

    The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans (2nd edition, HHS 2018) state that greater volumes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity are associated with reduced sleep latency (taking less time to fall asleep), improved sleep efficiency, improved sleep quality and more deep sleep. They add that the improvements are also reported by people with insomnia and obstructive sleep apnea.

    A meta-analysis by Kredlow and colleagues (2015) separates single sessions from regular training. It found that acute exercise has small beneficial effects on total sleep time, sleep onset latency, sleep efficiency and slow wave sleep, and a moderate beneficial effect on wake time after sleep onset. Regular exercise had small beneficial effects on total sleep time and sleep efficiency, small-to-medium effects on sleep onset latency and moderate effects on sleep quality. The authors report that the effects were moderated by factors including time of day, but the abstract does not say which time is best.

    Does the Time of Day Change the Picture?

    A 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis by Stutz, Eiholzer and Spengler tested the advice to avoid evening exercise. It reports that current recommendations advise against exercising in the evening because of potential adverse effects on sleep, but that the studies reviewed do not support the hypothesis that evening exercise negatively affects sleep. Evening exercise slightly increased slow-wave sleep (by 1.3 percentage points) and rapid eye movement latency (by 7.7 minutes). The review adds that sleep-onset latency, total sleep time and sleep efficiency might be impaired after vigorous exercise ending one hour or less before bedtime, and that all significant moderating effects disappeared after one study was removed.

    Health bodies stay cautious. The NHLBI advises avoiding intense exercise in the hour before bed (NHLBI healthy sleep habits). The NHS suggests avoiding exercise in the 4 hours before bed (NHS Every Mind Matters). A reasonable approach is to keep hard sessions earlier and finish evening workouts with something lighter.

    There is a separate line of research on the body clock. In a study by Youngstedt and colleagues (2019) of about 100 adults given one hour of moderate treadmill exercise at different times, the largest phase advances (earlier melatonin timing) corresponded to about 7 a.m. and 1 p.m. to 4 p.m., and delays corresponded to 7 p.m. to 10 p.m. This shows exercise can nudge circadian timing. It does not show that morning exercise gives better sleep.

    How Much Exercise?

    The Physical Activity Guidelines recommend at least 150 to 300 minutes a week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity, for substantial health benefits. The NHS also gives 150 minutes of exercise a week as the amount recommended for adults. These are general health targets and not sleep-specific prescriptions. The guidelines connect higher activity volumes with better sleep measures, but they do not name a minimum for sleep.

    Exercise and Insomnia

    The NHS lists exercising regularly during the day among its insomnia tips (NHS insomnia page). A 2024 umbrella review and network meta-analysis concluded that exercise had a positive effect on relieving insomnia and improving sleep quality, but it drew on systematic reviews rated as low or very low methodological quality. The NHLBI says cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is usually recommended as the first treatment option for long-term insomnia.     What the Research Does and Does Not Show

    • It supports regular physical activity as a habit linked to better sleep, with small to moderate effects.
    • It does not show that one time window (morning, afternoon or evening) is clearly best. The hour ranges in the headings above are common conventions, not findings from the studies we read.
    • It does not show that evening exercise is harmful for everyone, or that it is safe for everyone. The 2019 meta-analysis flagged vigorous exercise ending an hour or less before bed as the case where sleep might be impaired.
    • The studies measured groups. Your own response may differ, so tracking how you sleep after different workout times is more informative than any rule.

    When To Talk to a Clinician

    The NHLBI says that if not getting enough sleep is affecting your daily activities, you should talk to your doctor, and that insomnia is considered chronic when it occurs 3 or more nights a week and lasts 3 months or longer (NHLBI insomnia diagnosis). The NHS says to see a GP if changing your sleeping habits has not helped your insomnia, if you have had trouble sleeping for months, or if your insomnia is affecting your daily life in a way that makes it hard for you to cope.
