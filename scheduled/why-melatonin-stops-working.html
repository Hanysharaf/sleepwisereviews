Why Melatonin Stops Working After a Few Weeks

It's one of the most common complaints in sleep forums: melatonin worked great for the first two or three weeks, then quietly stopped doing anything. The usual explanation people reach for is "tolerance" - the body adapted, the receptors shut down, you need a bigger dose or a break. The actual research tells a more useful and less dramatic story.

    The Pattern Everyone Describes

    It shows up the same way almost every time: someone starts melatonin, sleep improves within the first few nights, and they tell themselves they've finally found something that works. Then, somewhere between week two and week six, the effect quietly disappears. They are not asleep faster. They don't feel noticeably different than before they started. The assumption that follows is almost automatic - "my body built up a tolerance."

    That assumption borrows its logic from a different category of drug entirely. Tolerance in the classic sense - needing more and more of something to get the same effect - is well documented for benzodiazepines, Z-drugs like zolpidem, and even over-the-counter antihistamines like diphenhydramine, which can lose effectiveness within days. Melatonin is a different kind of molecule doing a different kind of job, and the "stopped working" pattern usually has a different explanation.

    Reason 1: The Job It Was Doing May Already Be Done

    Melatonin is not a sedative. It is a chronobiotic - a signal that tells your circadian clock what time of "biological night" it is, and nudges the timing of that clock earlier or later depending on when you take it. When melatonin is used correctly, for a correctly circadian problem - jet lag, a delayed sleep phase, a night-shift schedule - its job is to move your sleep window to a new position and then stop being necessary.

    What this looks like in practice: if you used melatonin to shift your bedtime two hours earlier and it worked, there is no further shift left to make. Continuing to take it nightly afterward won't produce a bigger or more noticeable effect, because the clock has already arrived where it was going. People read the absence of a continuing dramatic effect as "it stopped working," when a more accurate description is "it finished."

    This is the single most under-discussed explanation for the "stopped working" complaint, because it reframes the experience as a sign of success rather than failure.

    Reason 2: Expectation Was Doing More Work Than the Molecule

    Insomnia is one of the conditions most susceptible to placebo response in all of medicine. Research on placebo treatment for insomnia has found subjective sleep improvements averaging around 61% relative to baseline in placebo arms, and that the effect is driven almost entirely by what people expect the treatment to do rather than any active ingredient. That expectancy effect is strongest in the first few weeks of a new intervention - exactly the window in which most people report melatonin "working."

    Melatonin's own measured effect on sleep, separate from expectation, is real but modest: clinical meta-analyses consistently find small effect sizes for time-to-fall-asleep, on the order of minutes, not hours. When the novelty and expectation fade - typically a few weeks in, for almost any intervention - what's left is melatonin's actual, modest, physiological effect. That can feel like "nothing," even though nothing about the mechanism has changed.

    Reason 3: It May Have Been the Wrong Tool From the Start

    The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2017 clinical practice guideline on pharmacologic treatment of chronic insomnia gives a weak recommendation against using melatonin for general sleep-onset or sleep-maintenance insomnia in adults. The evidence melatonin is actually strong for is narrower: circadian rhythm problems like jet lag, shift work disorder, and delayed sleep phase - not the everyday experience of lying awake with a racing mind.

    What gets misread as "stopped working": if the real driver of your sleep trouble is conditioned arousal - your brain has learned to associate bed with wakefulness and worry rather than sleep - melatonin was never treating that mechanism. A stressful few weeks, a schedule disruption, or just the normal waxing and waning of chronic insomnia can resurface the real problem. It looks like the supplement failed. More accurately, it was never the right category of fix.

    This is also why melatonin and CBT-I (cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia) aren't competing options - they're aimed at different problems. If lying awake rather than clock misalignment is the issue, CBT-I is the evidence-based next step, not a higher dose.

    Reason 4: Most OTC Doses Already Maxed Out the Response Curve

    Melatonin's dose-response relationship flattens out close to the amount your body produces naturally - in the 0.3-0.5mg range. Most melatonin sold in US pharmacies comes in 3mg, 5mg, or 10mg tablets, which is 10 to 30 times that physiological range. That means a lot of people start on a dose that's already past the point where more produces more effect.

    When the felt effect fades, the instinctive response is to take more. But if you were already on 5-10mg, there was no more room on the curve to begin with - increasing the dose mostly adds side effects (next-morning grogginess, vivid or unsettling dreams, a hungover feeling) without restoring anything that was lost, because nothing on the dose side was actually the limiting factor.

    What the Research Says About Real Tolerance

    None of this means tolerance is impossible - it means the evidence for it in humans, at normal doses, is much thinner than the Reddit-thread consensus suggests.

    • Receptor desensitization is real, but mostly shown in animal and cell models. Studies on the SCN (the brain's master clock) have found that melatonin's MT2 receptor can desensitize with repeated nightly exposure in rodent and cell-culture models. That's a genuine mechanism worth knowing about - it just hasn't been clearly shown to be the cause of lost effectiveness in humans taking typical doses.
    • The largest human long-term study found no tolerance at all. A 12-month open-label trial following 244 adults on nightly prolonged-release melatonin found no need to increase the dose over time, no rebound insomnia on stopping, and no measurable suppression of the body's own melatonin production.
    • The Sleep Foundation states plainly that melatonin does not cause physical dependence and that stopping it does not produce withdrawal or rebound insomnia - a meaningfully different profile from prescription sleep medication.
    • Individual metabolism varies. Some research links a weaker or fading response to melatonin to how quickly a person metabolizes it - fast metabolizers clear it from the bloodstream before it can do much, which looks identical to "tolerance" from the outside but has a different cause and a different fix (timing adjustment, not dose escalation).
    The honest summary: true pharmacological tolerance to melatonin, in the way it's commonly meant, is not well supported by human evidence at physiological doses. "Stopped working" is far more often the phase-shift completing, expectation fading, the wrong problem being targeted, or an already-maxed-out dose - all of which point to a different fix than "take more" or "take a break and come back stronger."

    What to Actually Do About It

    1. Check what you're actually taking. If you're on 3-10mg, you were likely past the effective range from day one. Dropping to 0.3-0.5mg, taken about two hours before your target sleep time, is the evidence-supported approach - see our full melatonin dosing guide for specifics.
    2. Ask what problem you were actually solving. Jet lag, a shifted schedule, or shift work are circadian problems melatonin is built for. Lying awake with your mind racing most nights is a different problem, and no dose of melatonin fixes it.
    3. If the original circadian issue is resolved, you may not need it anymore. A lack of continuing dramatic effect after a successful phase shift isn't failure - it's often the sign the job is done.
    4. If the real issue is chronic insomnia, look at CBT-I. It's the first-line recommended treatment and addresses the conditioned-arousal mechanism that melatonin doesn't touch.
    5. Don't chase the feeling with a higher dose. If 0.3-0.5mg isn't producing a noticeable effect, more won't either - it will mostly add side effects.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Why did melatonin stop working for me after a few weeks?

    Usually it is not true pharmacological tolerance. The most common causes are: the circadian phase-shift it was helping with already completed (so there is nothing left to correct), the early benefit was partly a placebo/expectancy effect that naturally fades, or the underlying problem was never a circadian one - in which case melatonin was never going to fix it long-term. A 12-month study of 244 adults on nightly prolonged-release melatonin found no dose escalation or loss of effect over time, which argues against classic tolerance as the default explanation.

    Does melatonin cause real tolerance like sleeping pills do?

    Not in the way benzodiazepines or Z-drugs do. The Sleep Foundation states melatonin does not cause physical dependence and stopping it does not produce withdrawal or rebound insomnia. Lab studies have shown melatonin's MT2 receptor can desensitize with repeated exposure in animal and cell models, but this has not been clearly demonstrated to explain lost effectiveness in humans at typical doses, and long-term human trials have not found a need to increase dose over time.

    Can taking more melatonin fix the problem when it stops working?

    No, and it often makes things worse. The dose-response curve for melatonin flattens out at physiological levels (around 0.3-0.5mg). Most US over-the-counter tablets already contain 10-30 times that amount, so the response curve has already maxed out before you even started. Taking more adds side effects - grogginess, vivid dreams, morning sluggishness - without adding benefit.

    How do I know if melatonin was ever the right tool for my insomnia?

    Melatonin has strong evidence for circadian timing problems - jet lag, shift work, delayed sleep phase. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2017 guideline gives a weak recommendation against using melatonin for general chronic insomnia, because the evidence for that use is thin. If your sleep problem is lying awake with a racing mind, not a misaligned body clock, melatonin was not addressing the mechanism, and CBT-I is the recommended first-line treatment instead.
